Rosa Martinez was nineteen when she left San Pedro, a small town in the mountains of southern Oaxaca, in 1986. She carried two suitcases and a hand-cranked sewing machine that had belonged to her mother. Her older brother was already working in a garment shop in Los Angeles and had promised her a place to stay. This essay follows Rosa's journey north and the thirty years she spent building a life on the strength of her skill with a needle. Her story shows how family networks, economic change in Mexico, and the demand for labor in the United States came together in one household.

## Why so many Oaxacans left in the 1980s

Rosa's departure came during a decade of economic crisis in Mexico. After the 1982 debt crisis, the peso collapsed, rural prices for corn and coffee fell, and government support for small farmers shrank. Historians of Mexican migration describe how these pressures pushed highland families toward cities and then across the border. At the same time, Los Angeles's garment industry was expanding and relied on immigrant women to sew. The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 offered a path to legal status for some long-term residents, but newly arrived workers like Rosa navigated a hard line between opportunity and risk.

## The journey, the machine, and a sense of home

Rosa's trip took nine days by bus and on foot. She remembers the sewing machine as the heaviest and most precious thing she owned. In Los Angeles, she took piecework at night and used the machine to hem curtains for neighbors. Over time she opened a small alterations shop, and the machine sat in its window. Today it still works. For Rosa, it ties the mountains of Oaxaca to the apartment where her grandchildren do their homework.

## Other stories of work and movement

Rosa's story echoes the experiences of garment workers who came before her, from Jewish and Italian women in New York's early twentieth-century sweatshops to Chinese seamstresses in San Francisco. Like them, she turned a skill into a foothold. Unlike many earlier migrants, though, she crossed a border that had become increasingly policed.

## What the machine taught me

Writing about Rosa taught me that migration history lives in ordinary objects. A sewing machine turns out to be a record of labor, memory, and love that no document could hold.
