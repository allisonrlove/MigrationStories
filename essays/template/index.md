In Fall 1929, my grandfather Wing Ak Fong left his wife and infant son for the United States. Due to China's political and economic instability, he and his four brothers departed for the United States to make new lives for their families. Initially prevented from becoming a U.S. citizen due to Chinese Exclusion laws, my grandfather began a laundry in the West side of Chicago, through which he would support additional family and village members' chain migration to the States.  After his wife See Chew joined him in the US, they would raise their eight children in the back of the laundry, see their church leave the city due to white flight, and navigate the class politics of Chinatown. Their children who remained in Chicago would continue working for their father's laundry even as they pursued educational opportunities.

My grandfather's decision to migrate to the United States with his brothers followed a tradition of chain migration from Taishan County in Guandong Province that began in the mid-19th century. Villagers throughout Guangdong Province in Southern China had been beset with rising rents as the Chinese government sought to to reject increasing European attempts to sell their goods in China. The gunboat diplomacy of Great Britain after the Opium Wars had overpowered China's defenses, and European powers and the United States all demanded concessions of access and exorbitant financial penalties. These penalties were passed onto landlords who in turn raised rents on peasants; a series of natural disasters exacerbated the hardship, resulting in what historian Jon Gjerde calls "a radical attempt to conserve" a traditional way of life. Chinese who had some means paid their fares to North America; those Chinese who did not have resources indentured their future labor in exchange for passage to the harsher working conditions of plantations in Latin America.  The large numbers of Toishan emigrants resulted in funds being sent back to the villages, which in turn inspired more outmigration. These remittances raised the quality of life in the villages but also crated greater pressures for younger men to migrate to earn wages in order to keep up with the increased cost of land and goods. Geopolitical developments in the late 1920s also contributed to a desire to migrate. Japan had undergone concerted modernization modeled on Western imperialism.
