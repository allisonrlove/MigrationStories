Linh Tran was twelve when her family fled Vietnam by boat in 1979. Today she keeps a shoebox of letters that her grandmother wrote to relatives scattered across three continents. This essay follows those letters from a refugee camp in Malaysia to a church basement in Saint Paul, Minnesota. It shows how sponsorship, language, and long-distance writing helped one family build a new life in a very cold place.

## Refugee policy and the Vietnamese exodus

After the fall of Saigon in 1975, more than a million people left Vietnam over the following two decades. The Refugee Act of 1980 created a formal system for resettling refugees in the United States, and churches and community groups often served as sponsors. Minnesota became home to one of the largest Hmong and Vietnamese communities in the country. The Tran family was placed there by a Lutheran congregation that offered housing, winter coats, and English lessons.

## The journey, the letters, and a new identity

The letters are written in Vietnamese on thin blue paper. They describe the camp, the voyage, and the first snowfall. Linh learned English in school while her grandmother learned it from television, and the letters slowly begin to mix both languages. For Linh, they show how identity is built in translation.
