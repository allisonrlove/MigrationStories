When the Singh family left their village near Lahore in August 1947, they were given one hour to pack. Gurpreet Singh, then eight years old, remembers his mother wrapping a brass kettle in a blanket. That kettle has since traveled to Amritsar, to London, and finally to an apartment in Queens, New York. This essay uses the kettle to trace three generations of movement across borders drawn by others. It argues that families remake home by carrying small, ordinary things from place to place.

## Partition and the long road to the United States

The 1947 Partition of British India displaced an estimated fifteen million people, one of the largest forced migrations in modern history. Punjab was divided between India and Pakistan, and millions of Sikhs, Hindus, and Muslims crossed the new line in fear. Many families later moved again. Changes to United States immigration law in 1965 ended national-origins quotas and opened doors for skilled workers and family members from South Asia. The Singh family's decision to move to New York in 1979 reflects both the trauma of Partition and the new opportunities created by that law.

## The journey, the kettle, and belonging

The kettle was used every morning in each new home. In London, it sat on a gas stove beside an English teapot. In Queens, it is still used on Sunday mornings when the family gathers. Gurpreet explains that the dents record each move. For him, belonging is not a single place but a set of rituals that can be repeated anywhere.

## Other stories of displacement

The Singh family's story parallels those of other refugees and diaspora communities studied in class, from Armenian survivors to Vietnamese boat families. In each case, objects stand in for the places left behind.
